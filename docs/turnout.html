Five million ballots: 71 percent of 2024, 93 percent of 2022, and the drop was steepest where Trump ran best

Turnout fell everywhere from the presidential year, as it always does in a midterm. But it fell least in the Philadelphia suburbs and the Harrisburg corridor and most in the rural T and the southwest, so the electorate that showed up was bluer than the one that elected Trump.

Measured by votes for governor, 2026 turnout was of the 2024 presidential vote and of the 2022 governor's vote. Those two ratios tell different stories. Against 2024 the state lost nearly three in ten voters. Against the last midterm it lost about one in fourteen, and in several eastern counties it lost none at all.

Where the voters went missing matters more than how many did. The chart below plots each county's 2026 vote as a share of its 2024 vote against how that county voted for president in 2024. What this can and cannot say

County totals cannot tell apart two explanations for the same 22-point swing: 2024 Trump voters who stayed home and 2024 Trump voters who voted for Shapiro. This page shows the first was large. The swing map shows the biggest swings were in the same working-class counties where turnout fell most, which fits the stay-at-home story, but it does not rule out persuasion. Precinct data will narrow it; a voter file would settle it. Treat the "it was turnout" reading as a hypothesis with evidence, not a result.

All 2026 counts are unofficial and will rise. Mail ballots are still being added in some counties, and provisional ballots, about one percent of the 2024 vote, are essentially absent so far. The turnout ratios on this page are therefore floors.
